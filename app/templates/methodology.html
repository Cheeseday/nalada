Everything scored on the site is reproducible from three public datasets - Our World in Data, the World Bank and Eurostat. The analysis notebooks in this project as well as every subjective choice (which metric, which weight, which threshold) is written down here so you can disagree with it precisely.

There are two key principles at work. First, consumption-based accounting is the foundation: a country's real carbon emissions are measured by what its residents ultimately consume, wherever they are produced, not just what its domestic factories emit. Second, the scoring is criteria-based - measured against fixed, real-world anchors, rather than against the other countries, so the score means the same thing no matter who else is in the table.

The decoupling question itself isn't new: Our World in Data found that many rich countries cut emissions even after accounting for trade. Nalada's contribution is to turn that finding into something measurable and verifiable - a per-country honesty verdict and a composite index that must pass a series of robustness tests before it can be trusted.

01

Measuring decoupling: the Tapio model

Chapter 2 begins with the flattering lens - a standard method that asks whether economic growth and emissions have come apart, but only within country borders.

The decoupling map in Chapter 2 uses the Tapio decoupling model, introduced by Finnish researcher Petri Tapio in 2005. It measures a decoupling elasticity - the ratio between the percentage change in an environmental pressure and the percentage change in the economic driver over the same period:

elasticity = (% change in CO₂) ÷ (% change in GDP)

Read it this way: for every percent the economy grew, how did emissions respond? Tapio split the continuum into eight stages; this project uses the four stages, that carry the GDP vs CO₂ story:

  • Strong decoupling - GDP rises while CO₂ falls (elasticity below 0). The goal.
  • Weak decoupling - both rise, but emissions more slowly (elasticity 0 to 0.8).
  • Coupling - emissions growth roughly corresponds to GDP growth (elasticity 0.8 to 1.2).
  • Recession - the economy shrank, so any emissions reduction is not counted as progress.

On the elasticity map, the bottom-right quadrant is strong decoupling: GDP up, territorial CO₂ down. Romania is the vivid case: its economy almost quadrupled since 1990 while territorial CO₂ more than halved - an elasticity far below zero, one of the deepest strong-decoupling readings in Europe. But the Tapio model only sees territorial emissions - what a country burns within its borders. It cannot tell a country that genuinely reduced its carbon footprint from one that simply moved its factories abroad. That "blind spot" is exactly what the next step corrects.

Method: Tapio, P. (2005). Towards a theory of decoupling: degrees of decoupling in the EU and the case of road traffic in Finland between 1970 and 2001. Transport Policy, 12(2), 137-151.

02

The honesty verdict: genuine, partial or fake

Turning the territorial picture into a judgement about who actually decoupled - the classifier that Chapters 2, 3 and 4 all read from.

Because the Tapio lens is territorial, a country can look decoupled simply by importing the things it used to make. The verdict fixes this with a single transparent ratio - the real-share of a country's reported cut. Both curves are indexed to 1990 = 100 and read at the latest year:

real-share = (100 - consumption index) ÷ (100 - territorial index)

In words: for every ton of territorial CO₂ a country reports cutting, how much is backed by a real reduction in consumption by its population? A ratio of 1.0 means the footprint fell just as much as the reported number; a ratio near 0 means the reported cut mostly moved abroad. The verdict then follows a short decision tree - first match wins:

  1. GDP didn't grow (index ≤ 100) → degrowth (e.g. Ukraine) - there's no growth to decouple from.
  2. The country exports more embedded CO₂ than it imports (trade share < 0) → net exporter (Poland).
  3. Territorial emissions barely moved (index ≥ 95) → no decoupling (Austria, Spain, Cyprus, Ireland).
  4. Otherwise, on the real-share: ≥ 0.70 → genuine; 0.30 to 0.70 → partial; below 0.30 → fake.

The two thresholds aren't round numbers: 0.70 and 0.30 both fall within natural gaps in the data (the countries cluster at 0.64 then jump to 0.79, and at 0.19 then jump to 0.41), so the split barely moves even if you slightly altered thresholds. The result across the 34 countries is 15 genuine, 6 partial, 7 fake, 4 no-decoupling, 1 net exporter and 1 degrowth. This classification is built once (in notebook 04) and stored in one table, so Chapter 2's verdict board, city analysis in Chapter 3 and leaderboard in Chapter 4 all use the same language.

Let me demonstrate three countries that make the ratio concrete. Germany's territorial emissions drop to 63% of their level in 1990 and its consumption drop to 64% - a real-share of 0.98, so nearly the whole reported cut is backed by a genuine fall in what Germans consume (genuine). Sweden's consumption dropped even faster than its territorial number, a real-share above 1.2 - the clear example of a country actually reducing its footprint rather than exporting it. Switzerland is a mirror image: its territorial emissions look admirable, but its residents consume so much more than the country produces that the ratio turns sharply negative (fake).

03

The Transition Performance Index

The key score. What it measures, how the five components are weighted, and - above all - why honesty matters almost as much as everything else combined.

A note on the name

The European Commission's Joint Research Centre publishes an official composite index also called the Transitions Performance Index - 28 indicators across economic, social, environmental and governance dimensions, covering the EU and 45 other countries. Nalada's TPI is an independent, far smaller-scope index with a different construction (Europe-only, weighted heavily on consumption honesty, and published with its own self-audit) and is not affiliated with or derived from it. The shared name is a collision of two obvious words.

The TPI ranks European countries on a scale from 0 to 100 based on how well they combine an honest, consumption-based emissions reductions with a clean energy grid, low absolute footprint and real prosperity. It blends emission reduction trends (how fast emissions fell) with current states (where a country stands today), because a fast cutter that still consumes 12 tonnes per capita has not finished the job.

Components and weights

ComponentWeightWhat it captures
Honesty 0.40 Consumption-CO₂ reduction per capita (a 60/40 blend of the long-run and since-2010 cuts) minus a penalty when territorial emissions decline significantly faster than consumption. May become negative.
Grid energy cleanliness 0.15 Carbon intensity of energy today (kg CO₂/kWh) - encourages the drive for sustainability.
Absolute footprint 0.15 Consumption CO₂ per capita now versus the destination, measured using a 2-tonne fair share.
Prosperity 0.15 60% absolute GDP/capita + 40% a positive-growth guard (only shrinking economies lose points).
Territorial cut 0.15 The classic headline reduction in territorial CO₂ per capita from the base year.
Momentum ±3 pct A small modifier (not a weight) for the recent consumption trend. Added after weighting.

Why honesty is 0.40

This is the most consequential decision in the project, and it follows straight from the project's thesis. If you build a climate league table based on territorial emissions only, you reward offshoring: a country that closes a steel mill and imports the steel looks cleaner, even though the same tonne of CO₂ is now emitted somewhere else on its behalf. Paying 40% of the score for honesty makes that trick unprofitable. The component measures the reduction in consumption-based CO₂ per capita - the number that follows the imports - and then subtracts a penalty if a country's territorial curve has dropped far faster than its consumption curve, which is the statistical sign of offshoring. It's the only component that can be negative, because dishonesty must actively cost the country a position. Switzerland is the test case: its territorial emissions look admirable, but its residents consume roughly three times than the country emits, so its honesty rating is deeply negative and it drops the country from a spotless reputation to around rank 30.

This weighting factor is deliberately assertive, but don't be too quick to think that it creates an artificial index - the results of the fairness referees tests tell us that the coefficients only clarify the story, and the leaders remain leaders no matter what.

Why the other four are 0.15 each

The remaining 0.60 is split evenly across four components, each guarding against a specific way the index might reward the wrong thing:

  • Grid cleanliness scores the grid's absolute intensity, not its improvement, so an already-clean country (Norway, France, Sweden) isn't punished for having no easy gains left.
  • Absolute footprint is the destination to decoupling's journey: a country can decouple beautifully and still consume far too much, and this component says so.
  • Prosperity uses a growth guard rather than a growth reward - any positive growth earns full marks, only a shrinking economy loses points - which kills the "catch-up jackpot" that would otherwise hand post-communist GDP collapses a high score.
  • Territorial cut still must be in the index - it's the number governments report and citizens recognise - but at 0.15 it can no longer carry the whole story by its own.

Three more rules that keep it fair

  • Per capita, everywhere. This takes into account the sharp population decline in Eastern Europe. So, the reducion in total emissions is not due to emigration.
  • Two base years, side by side. 2000 is the headline - a fair fight for all of Europe, while 1990 is shown as a contrast because it hands the post-Soviet economies a sort of profit from the early-1990s industrial collapse. Those economies rise above 1990 base and fall above the 2000 one, and the distance they fall is a measure of how much of their early "progress" was really the collapse of Soviet-era heavy industry rather than deliberate policy. The shift between the two rankings is itself a finding.
  • Missing data renormalises. Weights are redistributed based on a country's actual presence. a country needs at least a territorial reduction and an absolute footprint to be ranked. Where consumption data starts late (Norway, 2003), honesty is measured from that year for both curves, so the offshoring comparison remains consistent.

Flags, and two things I deliberately leave behind

  • ! data-quality (Ireland, Luxembourg, Malta) - the inputs themselves are distorted: Irish GDP is inflated by multinational profit-shifting, Luxembourg's per-capita denominators are broken by cross-border workers, Malta is tiny and volatile. These countries was excluded from the headline ranking, although was shown in the full one.
  • * accounting boundary (Norway) - the data is accurate but the frame is kind: the emissions from Norway's exported oil and gas are burned abroad, outside both territorial and consumption accounting. Norway remains in the ranking, at least until the data can be counted fairly.
04

The anchors, and why these numbers

A criteria-based scoring needs fixed criteria. Each component is scored on a scale from 0 to 100 using two real-world anchors chosen from physics and policy.

The anchors don't move when the sample changes, adding or removing a country never reshuffles anyone else's score. Many league tables do the opposite and normalise the data against the sample, grading each country relative to the best and worst performers in a sample; the leader is then marked on a curve, and the entire table can be shuffled when a country is added or removed. Fixed anchors helps to avoid that situation - a 55% cut is worth the same in a field of ten countries or fifty.

Honesty itself is a combination of 60% of the long-run consumption reduction and 40% of the since-2010 reduction, minus fake-decoupling penalty - that's why it can end up below zero.

05

Six fairness referees

A composite index is a stack of subjective choices - which components, how to weight them, where to put each anchor, how much to trust noisy inputs. These tests allow you to verify the relevance of the rating.

#RefereeWhat it stress-testsWhat it found
1Named scenarios Six deliberate weightings (equal, honesty-heavy, destination-, prosperity- and reduction-heavy). Sweden, Portugal and Finland stay in the top three under every one; rank ranges are tight (Sweden moves by ±1, the UK by ±1).
2Weight Monte Carlo 2,000 fully random weightings Sweden holds a top-5 place in 99.9% of them. Finland, Norway, Portugal are most often in the top 5. The chart below ilustrates top-12.
3Rank correlation + leave-one-out Drops each component entirely and re-ranks; compares to the normative order. Orders stay near-identical (mean Spearman 0.93). Only removing honesty moves things much (ρ 0.75) - it confirms honesty is the most significant component by design.
4Threshold jitter Every anchor jittered ±15%, 200 full rebuilds. The same five leaders stay in the top 5 in 100% of runs - robust to the anchors as well as the weights.
5Component redundancy Correlation between five component scores. The strongest pair correlates at r = 0.56 - it's below the 0.6 double-counting line, so each component measures different things.
6Input-noise propagation Consumption ±12% systematic + ±5% yearly, territorial ±3%. 300 rebuilds. Varies the data. Scores come out as 95% confidence bands with rank intervals. Sweden leads at 83.1, but Portugal, Finland, Norway, the UK, Estonia and Denmark all overlap it - so the honest headline is a leading group, not a single #1.

Two of these results are worth reading together. Referee 3 shows the order barely moves unless you remove honesty - which is the design working as intended, not a fault, because honesty is meant to be essential. And Referee 6 is why I never crowns a single winner: where the confidence bands overlap, the honest reading is a tie, so "a leading group" is a claim the numbers support and "Sweden is number one" is not.

06

The limits of this analysis

This story accuses others of flattering reviews, so it owes you its own caveats. Six things to keep in mind before trusting any single number.

Consumption figures are modelled. Consumption CO₂ is reconstructed with multi-regional input-output models that estimate the carbon embodied in trade. They inherit uncertainty from global trade tables and sector aggregation - they capture the trend, not a precise level. Referee 6 exists precisely to propagate this.

Europe is the easy case. The continent is deindustrialised and import-heavy, so it is primed to look good on a consumption lens. Without non-European comparators, the index cannot rule out that the whole leaderboard is successful offshoring at scale - the honest limit of a Europe-only study.

Small and trade-heavy economies are just set aside. Ireland's profit-shifted GDP, Luxembourg's cross-border workforce and tiny Malta distort per-capita comparisons, so they are flagged and held out of the headline rather than ranked as if the numbers were clean.

The honesty weighting is a choice. Scoring 40% on honesty is a deliberate decision, not a law of nature - though the referees show the leaders survive very different weightings, so it shapes the emphasis more than the winners.

Bunker fuels and land use are not included. International aviation and shipping, and land-use (LULUCF) emissions, are excluded because the standard consumption dataset doesn't include them. It is a real gap for both countries with developed aviation industries and countries with rich forest resources.

A per-capita lens. Every score is per person, which is fairer to citizens but understates the overall level of emissions from a particular country, so a big, moderately efficient nation can outscore a small, heavy-footprint one.

07

How to read the result

The defensible summary is not "Country X is number one." It is this:

A leading group - stable across 2,000 random weightings, ±15% anchor jitter and propagated input noise - clearly outperforms the rest of Europe on honest transition metrics; and even that group is not yet cutting consumption emissions at a Paris-compatible pace.

That is a narrower claim than a single winner, and a more honest one. It is what the numbers will actually bear - and holding the conclusion to what the numbers bear is the whole point of this chapter. It also keeps the sufficiency punchline in view: being the best-governed transition in Europe and being on track for a liveable climate are two different tests, and this index only measures the first.
